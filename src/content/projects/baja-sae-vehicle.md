## Overview

I co-founded the Baja SAE program at Santa Rosa Junior College and led it through its first semester. I've since transitioned into the Vehicle Dynamics Manager role, where I own suspension, brakes, and steering. The car is a from-scratch effort — no prior chassis to copy from, no institutional CAD library to lean on — which means the early semesters are as much about building the program as they are about building a car.

## First semester — structure from zero

The opening months were less car design and more program design. We needed everyone to understand the rules, the scope of each subsystem, and the cadence of decisions a team has to make to ship a competition vehicle on time. I spent that first semester sourcing equipment, building the team's design and review process, and laying out the chassis architecture so subsequent work had a foundation to bolt to.

## Vehicle dynamics — what I own now

Suspension, brakes, and steering. The current phase is heavy on research and iteration:

- Working through Milliken and Milliken (*Race Car Vehicle Dynamics*) chapter-by-chapter and translating what's there into our own design constraints.
- Reading public reports and writeups from other Baja teams — the FSAE/Baja community publishes far more than people realize, and pattern-matching across designs is a cheaper way to build intuition than running every experiment yourself.
- Defining the kinematic targets (camber curves, roll center migration, anti-dive/anti-squat) before any geometry gets locked in CAD.

## Design and iteration

We're in SOLIDWORKS, with 3D-printed mockups for fit checks and CNC-milled prototypes for parts that need to take load. The cadence is: kinematic target → SOLIDWORKS sketch driving the geometry → printed mock-up for clearance and packaging → milled hard prototype for fit and load testing. Iteration speed matters more than absolute fidelity right now — we'll learn more from three rough revisions than one polished one.

## Challenges and learnings

The hardest lesson so far has been pacing the team — early on I underestimated how much of the manager role is removing blockers for other people. Suspension design is also genuinely hard. Compromises stack: lower the roll center for stability, you raise the half-shaft angles; widen the track for grip, you can't tow it. Milliken keeps reminding me that there is no right answer, only the right answer for your driver and your event.

## Outcome

In progress. Targeting a kinematically-defensible front and rear geometry by end of next semester, with a driveable prototype by competition season.
